# Research Talk Slides

Help the user make a research talk that an audience can follow from the first slide. Use the user's actual project, evidence, audience, time limit, requested format, and existing deck as the source of truth. Deliver only the outline, deck, speaker notes, or critique the user requested.

This skill distills the user's *Presentation Structure Guide.pages* and *St Marys Rd.m4a* teaching recording. Read [source notes](references/source-notes.md) when tailoring an opening narrative or reviewing a deck against the two worked examples. Do not require the original files to be present for later use.

## Build the story

1. Write the research question in one clear sentence. Identify the field and context, why the question matters, what existing work achieves, the remaining gap, and the proposed contribution. If the project develops a tool or model, state its input and output in ordinary language.
2. Distinguish **significance** (why the question is worth studying or what understanding it could add) from **impact** (what successful work could enable in practice). Make only claims supported by the user's material or verified sources. State uncertainty where outcomes are prospective.
3. Build the slides in this sequence by default: context and motivation → significance and possible impact → prior work and gap → concise research question → proposed approach or tool at a high level. Let each slide set up the next. Keep this order when creating or revising a deck unless the user's material, audience, time limit, or explicit direction calls for a different order. For a full research talk, the opening often takes roughly 6–8 slides; a short talk may need fewer.
4. Give every slide one job. Write its title as one short, plain sentence that states what the slide shows or concludes; someone scanning only the titles should understand the deck's main argument. Pair it with the clearest evidence or figure and a spoken transition that makes the next slide feel necessary. If two adjacent slides do not connect, revise their order, content, or transition.
5. Build motivation around what the research could reveal or enable. In the recording's two examples, the speaker starts with a meaningful clinical or biological question, shows what current measurements or analyses miss, identifies an opportunity in available data, and only then introduces a computational approach. Explain why an improvement matters beyond outperforming an earlier method.
6. Test the argument backward: if the proposed model or tool worked perfectly, would its output actually answer the research question and support the claimed impact? If not, revise the question, output, or impact claim.
7. When the user asks for a full talk, continue through the requested methods, results, limitations, and conclusion.

## Make slides easy to absorb

- Start at the level the audience knows; introduce technical terms only when needed. Shift emphasis for different disciplines or levels of expertise.
- Prefer a relevant, readable figure over dense prose. Keep words sparse enough to scan while listening. In slide text, highlight a few meaning-bearing keywords or short phrases with bold, underline, or a contrasting color, applied consistently; someone scanning only those highlights should grasp the main action or finding. Keep each title understandable on its own.
- Put citations close to borrowed figures or claims. Identify paper sources clearly and link web images where appropriate. Check any scientific claim or figure against its source rather than relying on a remembered example.
- If speaker notes are requested, include concise transition sentences and cues for pacing and keyword emphasis. Recommend eye contact and an unhurried pace when coaching delivery; do not crowd the slides with performance instructions.
- Create and visually inspect the requested slide artifact with the available format-specific tools. Check text legibility, figure labels, citations, and whether the deck fits the speaking time.

## Review before delivery

Check whether a listener can answer, from the opening alone: What is the question? Why does it matter? What is missing from prior work? What will this project do? If a tool or model is proposed, what goes in and what comes out? Then scan only the slide titles and highlighted phrases: they should reveal the story in the intended order and what each slide adds. Check that each transition has a reason and the level of detail suits the audience.
